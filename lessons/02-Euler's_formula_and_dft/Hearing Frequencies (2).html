How can a computer tell whether a particular frequency is inside a sound?

A microphone only ever hands a computer a wiggly line of numbers — pressure over time. There's no label saying "this is a 300 Hz tone." To find out, the computer tries a trick: it tests one frequency at a time, spins the signal backwards at that rate, and checks whether the result piles up in one direction or cancels itself out. That's the whole idea behind the Discrete Fourier Transform. Build a signal below and watch the test happen.

04 The equation behind it

Everything above is just this formula, run once per test frequency:

X[k]  =  Σn=0N−1  x[n] · e−i2πkn/N
x[n] = the n-th sample  ·  N = 64 samples in our window  ·  k = which frequency bin we're testing (bin k ≈ k × 50 Hz here)

The term e−i2πkn/N is where the "spinning" comes from. Euler's formula turns that exponential into something you can actually plot:

e−iθ  =  cos(θ) − i·sin(θ)
a point moving clockwise around the unit circle as θ grows

Multiplying each sample x[n] by this spinning point is exactly the "spun samples" plot in section 02. If the signal already oscillates at the tested rate, the spin exactly undoes it and the points line up in one direction, so the sum X[k] grows large. At any other rate, the points keep rotating relative to the signal and average out near zero over the N samples. Summing over every sample n — then trying every k — is the entire Discrete Fourier Transform.

x[n] — one sample of sound N — how many samples we look at k — which frequency we're testing X[k] — how strongly that frequency is present
